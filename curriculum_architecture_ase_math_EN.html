Spanish ASE Math Curriculum Architecture: A Guide for Faculty

City Colleges of Chicago — Adult Education · Levels 1–4 · Brightspace D2L + MyOpenMath
Professional development companion document. Reference example: Level 2, Unit 1.

What you will find in this document

This material captures what was presented during the sixty-minute session. It does not replace the discussion: it complements it and lets you revisit any points that call for review.

The central claim is simple: all four levels share a single architecture. Anyone who masters the structure of one level can move to any other without relearning the platform, and a student who advances loses no time reorienting.

1

The starting point

▾

Who our student is

The Spanish-speaking adult preparing for the GED in Spanish arrives in the classroom with three conditions that no textbook takes into account:

  • They have been out of the school system for years or decades. The mathematics they remember is fragmented, and what remains is often memorized rules with no understanding behind them.
  • They study after a full workday. The available cognitive load is limited, and so is their time.
  • Quality materials, practice generators, and interactive tools are, for the overwhelming part, in English.

Design consequence: the curriculum cannot be a translation of a course designed for full-time adolescents. It must be built from the ground up for an adult with little time, abundant life experience, and a damaged relationship with the subject.

The architecture of the four levels

▾

A single skeleton across the four levels

Levels 1, 2, 3, and 4 are not four independent courses built to different criteria. They share exactly the same structure:

96hours per level
(48 synchronous + 48 asynchronous)

812-hour topics
(≈ 2 weeks each)
34-hour lessons
per topic
4components
per lesson

Added to this, at every level, is the same objectives and skills page at the opening of each unit, the same logic of quizzes split by skill, and the same accessible styling system.

Why it matters: the instructor teaching Level 2 today can take on Level 4 next semester without relearning the platform. And the student who advances immediately recognizes where everything is. Structural coherence is not an aesthetic preference: it is time saved and anxiety not created.

Anchoring to the NRS

Each level corresponds to a band of the National Reporting System Educational Functioning Levels. The bands overlap deliberately:

Correspondence between course levels and NRS levels.
Course level NRS Dominant mathematical focus
Level 1NRS 1–2Whole numbers, fractions, decimals, measurement, basic graphs
Level 2NRS 2–3Signed integers, order of operations, ratios, percentages, transitional geometry, descriptive statistics
Level 3NRS 3–4Linear algebra, radicals, scientific notation, Pythagorean theorem, similarity, compound probability
Level 4NRS 4–5 (GED)Polynomials, factoring, quadratics, systems, functions and modeling

The overlap is intentional. A student who advances does not fall through the cracks: the next level picks up as integrated review what the previous one taught as new content.

Anchoring to standards and SLOs

Every topic in the curriculum plan explicitly declares:

  1. Its Illinois ABE/ASE Mathematics Content Standards (ICCB, February 2021 revision), with the corresponding Standard Number code.
  2. Its Student Learning Outcomes from the official course syllabus.
  3. Its section of the sixteen-week Topical Outline.

This is not compliance documentation added at the end. It is the scaffolding on which the decision of what enters each level, and in what order, was made.

An empty cell is information too

The content-sequence document presents each subtopic in a table spanning the four levels. A cell can have one of three states:

InstructionThe content is taught as new material, with assigned hours and declared standards.
Integrated reviewThe content is not taught anew: it is applied within a later topic that requires it.
Empty cellThe content is not addressed at that level. And that absence is a decision, not an oversight.

Reading a full row reveals the entire life of a piece of content: where it is born, where it is consolidated, and where it is taken as mastered. Content does not appear and disappear at random.

3

Anatomy of a unit

▾

We take as our example Unit 1 of Level 2: "Signed integers: the number line and the four operations" (weeks 1–2, 12 hours).

The first thing the student sees: objectives and skills

Every unit opens with an objectives page. It is not an administrative formality: it answers the question the adult always asks — "what is this good for?" — before any effort is demanded.

  • A contextualization paragraph that anchors the content in real experience (below-zero temperatures, debts, depth below sea level).
  • An objectives accordion: what the student will be able to do.
  • A skills accordion: what they will develop in order to do it.

The distinction between objective and skill is not rhetorical. The objective is the observable outcome; the skill is the capacity that makes it possible and that transfers to other content.

The four components of each lesson

Every four-hour lesson declares the same four components, across all levels and all topics:

The four required components of every lesson.
Component What it specifies
Materials and resourcesManipulatives, tables, the TI-30XS calculator, MyOpenMath exercises, textbook references, and — crucially — the modality: the specific sequence of representation (real context → visual model → formal algorithm).
Guided practiceAn activity with its own name and a recognizable context, with its synchronous and its asynchronous parts.
Quiz or assignmentNumber of questions, distribution by skill, time, attempts allowed, and whether a calculator is permitted.
RubricChecklist or analytic rubric with explicit criteria and performance levels.

One skill, one quiz

This is the point where the Brightspace implementation deliberately departs from the curriculum plan, and it is worth pausing on.

The plan groups content by class hour: one lesson equals four hours. But a four-hour lesson can cover two or three distinct skills. Assessment, therefore, is organized not by lesson but by skill.

Splitting of assessment in Unit 1 of Level 2.
Lesson (curriculum plan) Assessment (Brightspace implementation)
Lesson 1.1 · The number line, absolute value, and opposites Quiz 1.1.a · Signed integers: number line
Quiz 1.1.b · Signed integers: absolute value
Lesson 1.2 · Addition and subtraction of signed integers Quiz 1.2 · Addition and subtraction of signed integers
Lesson 1.3 · Multiplication and division of integers; applications Practice: Build fluency with the rule of signs
Quiz 1.3.a · Multiplication of integers
Quiz 1.3.b · Division of integers
Quiz 1.3.c · Multiplication and division word problems

Three reasons to split:

  1. Clean diagnosis. If a student scores 6 out of 10 on a quiz that mixes the number line and absolute value, the instructor cannot tell where the difficulty lies. One quiz per skill does tell.
  2. Lower cognitive load. Short, focused assessments — not twenty-five-question exams mixing everything together at the end of a workday.
  3. Surgical remediation. If a student fails Quiz 1.3.b, they review division of integers. There is no need to repeat the entire lesson.

The lettered numbering (1.1.a, 1.1.b, 1.3.a…) preserves traceability: anyone can see at a glance which lesson each assessment belongs to, and the Brightspace gradebook stays ordered with no additional intervention.

The discussion forum as asynchronous practice

The forum is not decorative filler. It serves a function the self-graded quiz cannot: it requires the student to produce a problem, not merely solve one, and to read critically a peer's work.

Standard forum structure across all units:

  1. The student finds or constructs a real situation related to the content.
  2. They post it to the forum as a new entry, with their solution.
  3. They respond to at least one peer's post with their own point of view.
  4. They review the responses they receive.

The instructions sit inside a collapsed accordion: anyone who already knows them does not have to scroll past them every time they enter.

Brightspace and MyOpenMath: the classroom and the gym

▾

The two platforms do not compete. They do different things, and confusing them produces duplicated work.

Division of functions between the two platforms.
Brightspace D2L — the classroom MyOpenMath — the gym
Expository content: interactive lessons, objectives, materials. Algorithmic practice: each student receives a different version of the same exercise.
Formal assessment: quizzes by skill, with controlled attempts and time. Unlimited repetition with immediate feedback and no penalty.
Interaction: forums, discussion, student writing. Self-grading: consumes no instructor time.
Official gradebook and institutional reporting. Detection of the specific difficulty before it reaches the quiz.

The practical rule: if the activity requires the student to build, explain, or be formally assessed, it goes in Brightspace. If it requires drilling until automatic, it goes in MyOpenMath. The common mistake is using Brightspace for repetitive practice: it turns into endless manual grading.

The student's typical week

1. Live classReal context, visual model, formalization. The instructor leads.
2. Guided practiceA named activity, in pairs or groups, within class.
3. MyOpenMathUnlimited asynchronous drilling, with step-by-step feedback.
4. Quiz in BrightspaceOne skill, defined time, graded to the book.
5. ForumTheir own production and critical reading of a peer's work.
5

UDL is not decoration

▾

Universal Design for Learning is often presented as a list of good intentions. In this curriculum it is a set of verifiable decisions, and many of them are checked with a tool, not with an opinion.

The three principles, applied

The UDL principles and their concrete expression in the course.
Principle How it is applied
Multiple means of representation Every concept is presented in at least two modalities: real context, visual model (number line, two-color counters, grid), and formal algorithm. The sequence is declared in each lesson's "Materials" component.
Multiple means of action and expression The student demonstrates learning through different channels: self-graded quiz, hands-on practice, written production in the forum, oral presentation in the final project.
Multiple means of engagement Contexts anchored in real adult life (loans, receipts, household budget, travel). Short assessments that reduce threat. Multiple attempts where pedagogically sound.

What is verified, not opined

  • Contrast. All text over color must reach a contrast ratio of 4.5:1 (WCAG AA). Light amber and orange tones fail with white text. Dark browns are used instead — #5C3900 reaches 10.29:1. This is measured, not eyeballed.
  • Heading hierarchy. The h1 → h2 → h3 sequence is never skipped. A screen reader navigates by headings; a skipped level leaves it without a map.
  • Tables. Every table carries its caption element (even when visually hidden) and the scope="col" attribute on each heading. Without them, a table is an unordered list of cells to anyone who cannot see it.
  • Decorative symbols. Ornamental emoji and icons carry aria-hidden="true": the screen reader should not read "pencil emoji" before every instruction.
  • Keyboard navigation. Every interactive element is reachable with Tab and activatable with Enter or Space, with a visible focus indicator.

Brightspace's Ally is an ally. Its accessibility indicator is not a bureaucratic obstacle: it is a free reviewer that catches precisely the defects that shut part of the student body out. All course material is built to reach 100%.

Brightspace technical constraints worth knowing

  • The Brightspace editor strips <label> tags. For interactive elements, <div> elements with click handlers are used.
  • Gradients and colors with transparency (rgba) are unreliable in critical CSS. Solid hexadecimal colors are used.
  • Native <details> and <summary> accordions work correctly and are accessible by default. This is the preferred solution.
6

Artificial intelligence in producing the course

▾

It is worth saying plainly: a good part of this curriculum's material was produced with the assistance of artificial intelligence. Hiding it would be dishonest, and presenting it as a magic wand would be too.

What the tool does well

  • Writing and debugging the code of the interactive pages, the exercise generators, and the import files.
  • Producing variants of a single exercise and verifying the consistency of the answers.
  • Applying the accessibility rules systematically to dozens of documents without fatigue or oversight.

What it does not do, and must not do

  • It does not decide what is taught or in what order. That is a curricular decision anchored in the standards, in the NRS, and in knowledge of the student body.
  • It does not know that the Spanish-speaking adult in Chicago has real experience with loans and receipts, and that this is a doorway into percentages. The instructor knows that.
  • It does not detect when a "real-world" problem rings false. It errs with confidence and good prose, which is the most dangerous way to err.
  • It does not replace review. Everything published is read, verified, and corrected.

The analogy I propose: no one would say an engineer "stopped being an engineer" for using a scientific calculator. The calculator computes faster and without arithmetic errors, but it does not decide which equation to set up or what the result means. The same holds here: the tool accelerates execution, but the design of the course — what is taught, in what order, for which student — is an entirely instructional decision.

In one sentence: the tool is an extension of the capacity to execute, not of judgment. It multiplies what the instructor has already decided; it does not decide for them. An instructor with good judgment and this tool produces far more, and better. An instructor without judgment produces far more, and worse.

Questions for discussion

The questions that opened the debate in the session, for anyone who wants to revisit them:

  1. In your level, is there a lesson that assesses two or three skills with a single quiz? How would you split it?
  2. What content in your level would benefit from a context anchored in real adult experience that is currently presented abstractly?
  3. Where are you doing manual grading of repetitive practice that could move to MyOpenMath?
  4. What element of your current material would not pass a contrast or heading-hierarchy review?
  5. On AI-assisted production: what concerns you, and what appeals to you?
