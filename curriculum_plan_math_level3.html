Curriculum Plan — Mathematics, Level 3

Spanish ASE Math 3 · AE 0170-0323 · City Colleges of Chicago
96 total hours (48 synchronous + 48 asynchronous) · 16 weeks · NRS Levels 3–4
Platform: Brightspace D2L · Prof. José M. Fernández, with Claude

How this plan is organized

Content is distributed across 8 major topics of 12 hours each (≈ 2 weeks per topic), following the Topical Outline v2 of the 16-week syllabus and the Student Learning Outcomes.

Each topic contains 3 lessons of 4 hours. For every lesson: 📐 Materials/Resources , 💬 Guided practice , ✅ Quiz/Assignment and 📊 Rubric .

Topics 1–3 complete advanced number sense (exponents, scientific notation, radicals). Topics 4–5 develop complete linear algebra (equations, inequalities, formulas). Topics 6–7 cover advanced spatial geometry, volume, and data analysis. Topic 8 closes with combinatorics, compound probability, and the culminating integrative project.

Topic 1 · Rules of exponents

Weeks 1–2 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: NS · SLO Number System 1 · Section 1.1 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 1.1 · The product rule, power of a power, and power of a product

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students apply the product rule (aᵐ · aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ), power of a power ((aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ), and power of a product ((ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ) to simplify exponential expressions; they check their results by expanding the powers.
Materials/Resources: cards for the three rules with a demonstration by expansion (a³ · a² = aaa · aa = a⁵); pattern table for "discovering" the rules before stating them; MyOpenMath: properties of exponents lesson. Modality: induction — expansion → pattern → rule → application.
Guided practice: "Discover the rule" — students expand 4 examples of each type (a³·a⁴, (x²)³, (2y)⁴) and write the simplified result, then generalize the rule. Pair activity: one student writes an expression, the other simplifies it using the correct rule and names it. Asynchronous: 12 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 12 questions in Brightspace — 4 on the product rule, 4 on power of a power, 4 on power of a product (including numerical and variable bases). No calculator. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: rules of exponents (3 criteria × 4 levels): applies the product rule correctly (adds exponents of the same base) · applies power of a power (multiplies exponents) · applies power of a product (distributes the exponent to each factor).
▶

Lesson 1.2 · The quotient rule, the zero exponent, and negative exponents

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students apply the quotient rule (aᵐ/aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ), justify why a⁰ = 1 (for a ≠ 0) by means of the quotient rule, define negative exponents as reciprocals (a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ), and rewrite expressions with negative exponents as positive fractions.
Materials/Resources: pattern of decreasing powers of 2 (2⁴=16, 2³=8, 2²=4, 2¹=2, 2⁰=?, 2⁻¹=?, 2⁻²=?) for "discovering" the zero and negative exponents through repeated division by 2; summary table of all 5 complete rules; MyOpenMath. Modality: the pattern as the engine of discovery — not merely stating the rule, but watching it emerge.
Guided practice: "The magic table" — students complete the table of powers of 3 downward to 3⁻⁴, then convert 10 expressions with negative exponents into positive fractions and vice versa. Verification activity: use the TI-30XS calculator to confirm that 2⁻³ = 0.125 = 1/8. Asynchronous: 12 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on the quotient rule, 2 on the zero exponent (what is its value?), 5 on rewriting negative exponents as positive fractions and vice versa. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: negative and zero exponents (3 criteria × 4 levels): applies the quotient rule by subtracting exponents · justifies a⁰ = 1 using the quotient rule · correctly rewrites a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ in both directions.
▶

Lesson 1.3 · Simplifying exponential expressions with multiple rules

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students simplify exponential expressions requiring two or more rules in sequence (e.g., (2x³y⁻²)⁴ / (4x⁻¹y²)) and express the result with positive exponents; they identify and correct common errors (distributing the power incorrectly, confusing addition of bases with multiplication).
Materials/Resources: table of the 5 exponent rules kept in view; common-error cards ("which one is wrong?"); MyOpenMath: complete simplification lesson; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: step-by-step solving, noting which rule is applied at each step.
Guided practice: "The exponent referee" — the instructor presents 6 solutions with planted errors; students identify the incorrect step and correct it. They then simplify 8 complex expressions, showing each rule applied. Asynchronous: 10 multiple-rule simplification exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on simplification with 2 combined rules, 4 with 3+ rules, 2 on identifying the error in a given simplification. The result must always use positive exponents. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: integrative exponent rubric (4 criteria × 4 levels): identifies and applies the correct rule at each step · shows the intermediate steps clearly · expresses the final result with positive exponents only · detects common errors in other people's simplifications.

Topic 2 · Scientific notation

Weeks 3–4 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: NS · SLO Number System 2 · Section 1.2 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 2.1 · Decimal ↔ scientific conversion and orders of magnitude

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students convert very large and very small numbers between standard decimal notation and scientific notation (a × 10ⁿ, where 1 ≤ a < 10); determine the order of magnitude; and compare quantities of different orders of magnitude (the distance to the Moon vs. the diameter of an atom).
Materials/Resources: cards with real scientific data (mass of the Sun = 1.989 × 10³⁰ kg; mass of the electron = 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg; Earth-Sun distance = 1.496 × 10¹¹ m); the "move the decimal point" rule connected to powers of 10; MyOpenMath. Modality: astonishing scientific data as motivation before the mechanics of conversion.
Guided practice: "The universe in numbers" — students convert 10 data points from space and biology (the mass of bacteria, the distance between galaxies) into scientific notation and order them from least to greatest. Activity: how many times larger is the Earth than a red blood cell? (dividing powers of 10). Asynchronous: 12 conversions in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on decimal → scientific (2 large, 2 small), 4 on scientific → decimal, 2 on comparing and ordering numbers in scientific notation. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: conversion to scientific notation (3 criteria × 4 levels): correctly determines the coefficient a (1 ≤ a < 10) · determines the correct exponent of 10 (positive for large numbers, negative for small ones) · compares and orders numbers in scientific notation correctly.
▶

Lesson 2.2 · Multiplication and division in scientific notation

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students multiply and divide numbers in scientific notation using the rules of exponents (operating on the coefficients separately from the powers of 10) and check that the result is in correct scientific notation (adjusting the coefficient if necessary).
Materials/Resources: 3-step procedure: (1) operate on the coefficients, (2) apply the exponent rules to the powers of 10, (3) check that 1 ≤ coefficient < 10 and adjust if it is not; TI-30XS calculator (EE function); MyOpenMath. Modality: first without a calculator to master the procedure, then verify with the TI-30XS.
Guided practice: "NASA calculations" — students solve 6 problems in scientific contexts: the speed of light × travel time to Mars, the mass of a molecule × Avogadro's number, etc. Adjusting the coefficient: what do I do if the coefficient comes out as 24.5? (24.5 × 10⁴ = 2.45 × 10⁵). Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on multiplication, 4 on division, 2 requiring adjustment of the coefficient. No calculator. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: operations in scientific notation (3 criteria × 4 levels): operates on the coefficients correctly · applies the exponent rules to the powers of 10 · adjusts the result to standard scientific notation when the coefficient falls outside the range [1, 10).
▶

Lesson 2.3 · Addition, subtraction, and applications in science and technology

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students add and subtract numbers in scientific notation (first making the exponents of 10 equal), solve applied problems combining all four operations in scientific notation, and use the TI-30XS calculator efficiently to check complex calculations.
Materials/Resources: rule for addition/subtraction: "first make the exponents equal, then operate on the coefficients"; 8 interdisciplinary applied problems (astronomy, cell biology, computing, global economics); MyOpenMath; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: connection with other subjects — every problem references a science or a real context.
Guided practice: "The scientific report" — in groups of 3, students solve one mini-problem from each area (space, biology, technology) using all 4 operations in scientific notation and present the results in a "data report" format. Asynchronous: Brightspace discussion — each student finds a scientific figure in scientific notation and shares it with its conversion.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on addition, 3 on subtraction (making exponents equal), 4 on mixed applications. TI-30XS calculator allowed for checking. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: integrative scientific notation rubric (4 criteria × 4 levels): makes the exponents equal correctly before adding/subtracting · operates on the coefficients without errors · presents the result in valid scientific notation · solves a scientific-context problem accurately.

Topic 3 · Irrational radicals and the real number system

Weeks 5–6 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: NS · SLO Number System 3–4 · Section 1.3 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 3.1 · Simplifying radicals: factoring out perfect squares

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students simplify square radicals by extracting perfect square factors (√72 = √36·2 = 6√2), use the property √(ab) = √a · √b, simplify radicals containing variables (√x⁴ = x²), and recognize when a radical is already in simplest form.
Materials/Resources: factor tree for decomposing the radicand; table of perfect squares up to 225; the product property of radicals; MyOpenMath: simplifying radicals lesson. Modality: factor first → identify pairs → extract → simplify; always verify with a calculator.
Guided practice: "Simplify, architect" — the architect needs to simplify design measurements that contain radicals (√50, √98, √200, √48, √75...). Students work in pairs: one factors, the other simplifies. Verification activity: calculate √72 with the TI-30XS (≈ 8.485) and compare it with 6√2 ≈ 6 × 1.414 = 8.485. Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 6 on simplifying numerical radicals, 4 on simplifying radicals with variables. No calculator. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: simplifying radicals (3 criteria × 4 levels): factors the radicand by identifying the largest perfect square · applies √(a·b) = √a · √b correctly · presents the result in simplified form with the coefficient outside the radical.
▶

Lesson 3.2 · Operations with radicals: addition, subtraction, and multiplication

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students add and subtract like radicals (the same irrational part: 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2); simplify first when radicals are not alike; multiply radicals using √a · √b = √(ab) and binomials containing radicals; and approximate irrational radicals to two decimal places.
Materials/Resources: analogy with like terms in algebra (3x + 5x = 8x ↔ 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2); table of permitted and non-permitted operations with radicals (√2 + √3 cannot be combined); TI-30XS calculator; MyOpenMath. Modality: the algebraic analogy before the radicals.
Guided practice: "Alike or not?" — students classify 10 pairs of radicals as "can be combined / cannot be combined" before operating. They then add, subtract, and multiply 8 expressions with radicals, simplifying before combining when necessary. Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 12 questions in Brightspace — 3 on identifying like radicals, 4 on addition/subtraction, 4 on multiplication, 1 on approximating to 2 decimal places. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: operations with radicals (3 criteria × 4 levels): simplifies before attempting to combine radicals · combines only radicals with the same irrational part · multiplies radicals correctly and simplifies the result.
▶

Lesson 3.3 · The real number system: rationals, irrationals, and the complete number line

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students classify numbers within the real number system (naturals ⊂ integers ⊂ rationals ⊂ reals; irrationals ⊂ reals); locate irrational numbers on the number line using approximations; justify why π and √2 are irrational; and convert repeating decimals into fractions to show that they are rational.
Materials/Resources: Venn diagram of the real number system (concentric circles); number line marked with the most common irrational numbers (√2 ≈ 1.414, √3 ≈ 1.732, π ≈ 3.14159); procedure for converting a repeating decimal into a fraction (0.333... = 1/3); MyOpenMath. Modality: the Venn diagram as a concept map before classifying examples.
Guided practice: "Classify and locate" — students receive 20 numbers (integers, fractions, radicals, π, decimals) and classify them in the Venn diagram, then locate the irrational ones on the number line. Conversion activity: demonstrate that 0.666... = 2/3 and that √2 cannot be written as a fraction. Asynchronous: 10 classification and placement exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on classifying within the real number system, 3 on locating numbers on the number line, 3 on converting a repeating decimal into a fraction. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: the real number system (3 criteria × 4 levels): correctly classifies a number in the most specific subset of the diagram · locates irrational numbers on the number line with the correct approximation · converts a repeating decimal into a fraction using the algebraic procedure.

Topic 4 · Linear algebra: variables, expressions, and equations

Weeks 7–8 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: EE · SLO Expressions & Equations 1–5 · Sections 2.1 and 2.2 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 4.1 · Variables, algebraic expressions, and translation

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students use variables to write algebraic expressions, translate verbal statements into algebraic language (and vice versa), simplify expressions using the distributive property and combining like terms, and evaluate expressions by substituting integer or fractional values.
Materials/Resources: verbal→algebraic translation table (twice, half of, three more than, decreased by, the square of); "like term" cards for visual identification; MyOpenMath: algebraic expressions lesson. Modality: both directions — algebraic→verbal and verbal→algebraic.
Guided practice: "The math translator" — 10 verbal phrases to convert and 10 expressions to describe in words. Then simplify 8 expressions by combining like terms with the distributive property. Asynchronous: 12 exercises in MyOpenMath including evaluation by substitution.
Quiz: 12 questions in Brightspace — 4 on translating verbal→algebraic, 4 on simplifying expressions, 4 on evaluating expressions with given values. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: algebraic expressions (3 criteria × 4 levels): translates correctly between verbal and algebraic language · applies the distributive property and combines like terms · evaluates the expression by substituting the correct value and applying PEMDAS.
▶

Lesson 4.2 · Solving linear equations: properties of equality and variables on both sides

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students solve linear equations in one variable by systematically applying the properties of equality (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division); solve equations with variables on both sides; and use the "isolate the variable" strategy in a methodical, verifiable way.
Materials/Resources: a visual balance scale as a metaphor for the equation; an ordered-step strategy (1. Distribute · 2. Combine like terms · 3. Move variables to one side · 4. Isolate the variable · 5. Check); MyOpenMath; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: the 5 steps as a mandatory checklist before giving the answer.
Guided practice: "The error detector" — the instructor shows 4 equation solutions containing sign or operation errors; students identify and correct them. They then solve 8 equations (4 simple, 4 with variables on both sides), showing every step. Asynchronous: 12 equations in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on simple equations (2–3 steps), 4 with variables on both sides, 3 on identifying the correct first step. Time: 20 min. Always check by substituting.
Rubric: linear equations (4 criteria × 4 levels): applies the correct property of equality at each step · moves the variables to the same side correctly · isolates the variable without sign errors · checks the solution by substituting into the original equation.
▶

Lesson 4.3 · Equations with fractional and decimal coefficients, and special cases

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students solve equations with fractional coefficients (by multiplying by the LCM to eliminate them) and with decimal coefficients (by multiplying by powers of 10), and identify the special cases: an equation with no solution (0 = 5 → a contradiction) and an equation with infinitely many solutions (0 = 0 → an identity).
Materials/Resources: the "clear the fractions" strategy — multiply both sides by the LCM before solving; "clear the decimals" — multiply by 10, 100, or 1000 depending on the decimal digits; examples of contradictions and identities; MyOpenMath. Modality: a proactive strategy — "clear first, solve second."
Guided practice: "How many solutions?" — students solve 10 equations of different types and classify the result as: one solution / no solution / infinitely many solutions. Each result is verified. Discussion: what real situation is modeled by an equation with no solution? Asynchronous: 10 equations of all types in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on equations with fractions, 3 with decimals, 3 on special cases (identifying the type of solution). Time: 25 min.
Rubric: advanced equations (4 criteria × 4 levels): eliminates fractions by multiplying by the LCM · eliminates decimals by multiplying by the correct power of 10 · solves the resulting equation without errors · identifies and classifies the special cases correctly.
▶

Topic 5 · Linear inequalities, literal formulas, and general strategy

Weeks 9–10 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: EE · SLO Expressions & Equations 6–8 · Sections 2.3 and 2.4 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 5.1 · Solving linear inequalities and graphing on the number line

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students solve linear inequalities in one variable, apply the critical rule of reversing the sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative number, graph the solution on the number line (open vs. closed circle; arrow to the left vs. to the right), and express the solution in interval notation and as an inequality.
Materials/Resources: side-by-side comparison of equation↔inequality (the same steps EXCEPT the sign-reversal rule); number line for graphing; table: ≤ and ≥ → closed point ●; < and > → open point ○; MyOpenMath. Modality: verify the sign-reversal rule with a concrete numerical example: 6 > 4 → what happens if I multiply both by -1? -6 > -4 is FALSE → it reverses: -6 < -4.
Guided practice: "What happens to the sign?" — students solve 6 inequalities, marking with an asterisk every time the sign changes and why. They then graph the 6 solutions on the number line. Connection: which values satisfy the inequality? (check by substituting 3 values: one inside, one at the boundary, one outside). Asynchronous: 10 inequalities in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on solving the inequality, 3 on identifying the correct graph, 3 on determining whether a value satisfies the inequality. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: linear inequalities (4 criteria × 4 levels): applies the same steps as with equations · reverses the sign when multiplying/dividing by a negative · graphs correctly (open/closed point, direction) · checks the solution with a test value.
▶

Lesson 5.2 · Inequalities requiring simplification, and word problems

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students solve inequalities that require distributing, combining like terms, and moving variables across sides; translate verbal statements into inequalities and solve them; and interpret the solution in the context of the problem (at most how many items can be bought? what is the minimum number of hours needed?).
Materials/Resources: vocabulary of verbal inequalities (at least, no more than, minimum, maximum, enough for, does not exceed); 8 real-context problems (budget, time, speed, temperature); MyOpenMath; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: identify the key words before writing the inequality.
Guided practice: "The monthly budget" — 5 financial planning problems modeled with inequalities: "If I earn $2,400/month and want my expenses not to exceed 80% of my income, how much can I spend at most on entertainment if I have already spent $X on rent and $Y on food?" Asynchronous: 8 word problems in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on inequalities requiring prior simplification, 4 on translating verbal→inequality and solving, 2 on interpreting the solution in context. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: applied inequalities (4 criteria × 4 levels): simplifies correctly before solving · translates the verbal statement into the correct inequality · solves the inequality without errors · interprets and communicates the solution in the context of the problem with units.
▶

Lesson 5.3 · Solving literal formulas for a variable and general problem-solving strategy

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students solve for a specific variable in literal formulas from geometry, physics, and finance (A = bh → h = A/b; I = Prt → P = I/(rt); d = rt → t = d/r; F = (9/5)C + 32 → C = (F-32)·5/9); and apply the general 4-step problem-solving strategy.
Materials/Resources: table of formulas frequently used on the GED (area, perimeter, volume, simple interest, distance-rate-time, °F↔°C conversion); 4-step strategy: (1) identify the variable to solve for, (2) isolate it using the properties of equality, (3) simplify, (4) check; MyOpenMath. Modality: "solving for a variable is solving an equation in which the variable is a symbol, not a number."
Guided practice: "The GED formula kit" — students solve for each variable in the 8 formulas most frequently found on the exam. They then apply each rearranged formula to a context problem. Connection activity: if F = ma and I want a, what do I need to know? (a link to basic physics). Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 6 on solving a given formula for a variable, 4 on applying the rearranged formula to a context problem. The original formula is provided. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: solving literal formulas (3 criteria × 4 levels): correctly applies the properties of equality to isolate the target variable · simplifies the result correctly (with a fraction if necessary) · uses the rearranged formula to solve an applied problem correctly.

Topic 6 · Advanced geometry: angles, the Pythagorean Theorem, and similarity

Weeks 11–12 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: G · SLO Geometry 1–3 · Sections 3.1, 3.2, and 3.3 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 6.1 · Angles: relationships and parallel lines cut by a transversal

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students identify and use relationships among angles: complementary (summing to 90°), supplementary (summing to 180°), and vertical (opposite and equal); apply the properties of angles formed by two parallel lines cut by a transversal (corresponding, alternate interior, same-side interior); and calculate unknown angles in geometric figures.
Materials/Resources: diagram of a transversal with parallel lines, with every angle numbered and its relationships indicated; protractor for measuring and verifying; angle relationship cards (equal vs. supplementary); MyOpenMath. Modality: measure first with a protractor → discover the relationships → formalize them → apply them without measuring.
Guided practice: "The surveyor" — students receive 6 diagrams of parallel streets crossed by a transversal street and must calculate all unknown angles from one given angle. Construction activity: draw their own transversal diagram with a ruler and protractor, label the 8 angles, and classify their relationships. Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on complementary/supplementary angles, 4 on angles formed by a transversal, 3 on calculating angles in triangles (sum = 180°). Time: 20 min.
Rubric: angles (3 criteria × 4 levels): identifies the type of angle relationship correctly · applies the correct property (equal, supplementary, sum of 180°) · calculates the unknown angle without arithmetic errors.
▶

Lesson 6.2 · The Pythagorean Theorem: demonstration, applications, and converse

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students apply the Pythagorean Theorem (a² + b² = c²) to calculate the unknown side of a right triangle; use the converse (if a² + b² = c², then the triangle is a right triangle) to verify whether a triangle is a right triangle; and solve applied problems in 2D and 3D (distance in the plane, the height of a building, the length of a ramp).
Materials/Resources: visual demonstration of a² + b² = c² with squares constructed on each side; Pythagorean triples (3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25); TI-30XS calculator; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: visual demonstration with paper squares → formula → calculate the hypotenuse → calculate a leg → verify with a Pythagorean triple.
Guided practice: "The builder" — 8 real construction problems: how long is the diagonal of a 2 m × 90 cm door? how much cable do I need to anchor a 15 m antenna 8 m away from its base? is a lot with sides of 30 m, 40 m, and 50 m a right-angled lot? Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath, including 2 using the converse.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on calculating the hypotenuse, 3 on calculating a leg, 2 on verifying whether a triangle is a right triangle (the converse), 2 on contextual applications. Calculator allowed. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: the Pythagorean Theorem (4 criteria × 4 levels): correctly identifies the hypotenuse and the legs · sets up the equation a² + b² = c² correctly · calculates the unknown side by solving and taking the square root · applies the converse correctly to classify the triangle.
▶

Lesson 6.3 · Similar figures and scale drawings (Thales' Theorem)

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students identify similar figures (equal angles, proportional sides), set up and solve proportions to find unknown sides in similar figures, interpret and use the scale of drawings (maps, construction plans), and apply Thales' Theorem to find lengths from parallel lines and transversals.
Materials/Resources: pairs of similar triangles with all their angles and sides (some unknown); a map of Chicago with its scale; a simple scale construction plan; MyOpenMath; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: begin with visually similar figures before the algebraic proportion.
Guided practice: "The architect and the cartographer" — students (1) use the map of Chicago to calculate real distances between points; (2) calculate the height of a building from its shadow and that of a reference pole (Thales); (3) find sides in 3 pairs of similar triangles. Asynchronous: 8 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on identifying and calculating sides in similar figures, 3 on interpreting scale (map or plan), 4 on Thales' Theorem in context (shadow, mirror, pinhole camera). Time: 25 min. Calculator allowed.
Rubric: similarity and scale (3 criteria × 4 levels): verifies similarity by checking the proportionality of the sides · sets up and solves the proportion for the unknown side · applies the scale of the drawing correctly to convert to real measurements.

Topic 7 · Volume of solids and complex data analysis

Weeks 13–14 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: G · SP · SLO Geometry 4 · SLO Statistics & Probability 1–3 · Sections 3.4, 4.1 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 7.1 · Volume of prisms and cylinders

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students calculate the volume of rectangular and triangular prisms (V = base area × height) and cylinders (V = πr²h); solve for the height or the radius when the volume is known; and solve applied problems (the capacity of a tank, the amount of concrete, liquid storage).
Materials/Resources: physical models or 3D images of prisms and cylinders with real dimensions (a milk carton, a soda can, a propane tank); the formulas V = A_base × h; TI-30XS calculator (for π); MyOpenMath. Modality: first calculate the area of the base (recognizing its type), then multiply by the height.
Guided practice: "Construction and storage" — 6 real problems: how many liters fit in a cylindrical tank with a radius of 0.8 m and a height of 2 m? how many m³ of concrete do I need for a cylindrical column 3 m tall and 30 cm in diameter? Inverse problem: given the capacity and the height, find the radius. Asynchronous: 8 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on the volume of prisms, 4 on the volume of cylinders, 2 on solving for the height or the radius. Calculator allowed. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: volume of solids (4 criteria × 4 levels): correctly identifies the base and the height of the solid · calculates the area of the base without errors · applies V = A_base × h correctly · states the result in the correct cubic units (cm³, m³, L).
▶

Lesson 7.2 · Box-and-whisker plots

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students construct a box-and-whisker plot from an ordered data set by identifying the minimum, Q1, Q2 (the median), Q3, and the maximum; calculate the interquartile range (IQR = Q3 − Q1); identify outliers using the 1.5×IQR rule; and interpret the skew of the distribution from the box.
Materials/Resources: 5-step procedure for constructing the box (order → find the median → find Q1 and Q3 → draw the box → draw the whiskers); grid paper; real data sets of 10–15 values (temperatures, salaries, grades); MyOpenMath. Modality: construct on grid paper before using digital tools.
Guided practice: "The team's grades" — students construct box plots of the grades of two groups (A and B) and compare them: which group has greater variability? which has the higher median? are there outliers? Activity: given the plot, what can and cannot we know? Asynchronous: 6 construction and interpretation exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on calculating Q1, Q2, Q3, and the IQR, 3 on identifying outliers, 4 on interpreting a given plot (what is the median? what is the range? is there skew?). Time: 20 min.
Rubric: box plots (4 criteria × 4 levels): orders the data and correctly identifies the 5 key values · calculates the IQR and uses the 1.5×IQR rule for outliers · constructs the plot correctly to scale · interprets the symmetry/skew of the distribution.

Lesson 7.3 · Histograms: constructing, interpreting, and comparing distributions

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students construct a histogram from a frequency table, describe the shape of the distribution (symmetric, skewed right, skewed left, unimodal, bimodal), compare two distributions using a histogram and a box plot simultaneously, and choose the most informative representation for a given context.
Materials/Resources: the key difference between a bar graph and a histogram (adjacent bars in a histogram = continuous data); frequency table for constructing the histogram; 3 histograms with different shapes to analyze; MyOpenMath; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: construct from the table → analyze the shape → compare with the box plot of the same data set.
Guided practice: "The classroom profile" — students collect their own ages (or simulated heights) and construct a histogram with 5-year (or 5 cm) intervals. They then construct the box plot of the same data set and compare what information each graph provides. Discussion: when do I prefer the histogram? when the box plot? Asynchronous: 4 constructions + 4 interpretations in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on constructing or completing a histogram, 4 on interpreting shape and distribution, 3 on comparing two distributions (histogram vs. box plot). Time: 25 min.
Rubric: histograms (4 criteria × 4 levels): constructs the histogram with the correct intervals and frequencies · describes the shape of the distribution with correct terminology · compares two distributions using at least two statistics · chooses and justifies the most informative graphical representation for the context.

Topic 8 · Combinatorics, compound probability, and the culminating integrative project

Weeks 15–16 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: SP · SLO Statistics & Probability 4–5 · Sections 4.2 and 4.3 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 8.1 · The Fundamental Counting Principle, permutations, and combinations

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students apply the Fundamental Counting Principle to determine the total number of possible outcomes of successive events; calculate permutations (order matters: P(n,r) = n!/(n-r)!) and combinations (order does not matter: C(n,r) = n!/[r!(n-r)!]); and distinguish when to apply each one according to context.
Materials/Resources: tree diagram for the Fundamental Principle; comparison table: does order matter? → permutation vs. combination; the formulas P(n,r) and C(n,r) with the definition of n! (factorial); TI-30XS calculator (nPr and nCr functions); MyOpenMath. Modality: tree first → formula second → calculator to verify.
Guided practice: "The lottery and the committee" — in how many ways can I arrange 5 books on a shelf? (permutation) vs. in how many ways can I choose 3 books out of 5 to take on a trip? (combination). Classify 8 situations as permutation vs. combination before calculating. Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on the Fundamental Principle, 3 on permutations, 4 on combinations (including 2 on classifying the correct type). TI-30XS calculator allowed. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: combinatorics (3 criteria × 4 levels): applies the Fundamental Principle by multiplying the outcomes of each event · correctly determines whether the problem calls for a permutation or a combination · applies the correct formula without factorial errors.
▶

Lesson 8.2 · Compound probability: with and without replacement

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students calculate the probability of two compound events with replacement (independent: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)) and without replacement (dependent: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B|A)); construct probability tables and tree diagrams to visualize the sample space; and recognize the effect of replacement on the probabilities.
Materials/Resources: urn with colored chips for a physical experiment; table of differences: with replacement (the denominator does not change) vs. without replacement (the denominator decreases); two-level tree diagram; MyOpenMath; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: a physical experiment with the urn before the abstract formula.
Guided practice: "The bag of candy" — an urn with 4 red, 3 blue, and 2 green. Calculate P(red, then blue) with replacement vs. without replacement. Build the tree diagram for two draws. Extend to 3 events. Discussion: is the probability always greater with replacement? Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on probability with replacement, 4 without replacement, 2 on classifying and comparing (with or without replacement? which gives the greater probability?). Time: 20 min.
Rubric: compound probability (3 criteria × 4 levels): correctly classifies the type of event (independent/dependent) · applies the correct multiplication formula for that type · calculates without fraction errors and expresses the result as a fraction, a decimal, and a percentage.

Lesson 8.3 · Culminating integrative project + strategies for GED Mathematical Reasoning

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students present the integrative project "My Data Analysis" (a real data set + a box plot or histogram + statistical calculations + a written interpretation + at least one probability calculation), and answer 10 intermediate-level GED-style questions covering all the topics of the level.
Materials/Resources: integrative project template; GED Mathematical Reasoning reference sheet (the formulas provided on the exam); 10 intermediate-level GED-style questions (a mix of algebra, geometry, and statistics); Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: oral presentation of the project + practice with GED strategies (using the formula sheet, managing time).
Guided practice: "Sharing my analysis" — each student presents their project (3–4 min) with the graph, the statistics, and the interpretation. Classmates ask 1 question. Practice with 10 GED-style questions in pairs, discussing the solution strategy (not just the answer). Asynchronous: each student uploads their complete project to Brightspace and comments on two classmates' projects.
Quiz: Summative final assessment — 25 questions in Brightspace covering all topics: 5 on exponents and scientific notation, 5 on linear algebra (equations and inequalities), 5 on geometry (Pythagorean Theorem, similarity, volume), 5 on statistics (box plots, histograms, measures), 5 on probability and combinatorics. Time: 60 min. 2 attempts. TI-30XS calculator and the GED formula sheet are allowed.
Rubric: analytic rubric for the final project (5 criteria × 4 levels = 20 pts): (1) a real, well-described data set · (2) a correct graphical representation (box plot or histogram) with all labels · (3) correct calculation of at least 3 statistics (mean, median, IQR, or another) · (4) a written interpretation consistent with the data · (5) a correct probability calculation tied to the context of the project. The project grade is combined with the summative quiz grade.
📝 Pedagogical notes for review:
  • 50/50 distribution: each lesson = 2 synchronous hours + 2 asynchronous hours = 4 h. Total: 48 sync. + 48 async. = 96 h.
  • Algebra as the through-line: Topics 1–3 use algebra naturally (simplifying expressions with exponents, evaluating expressions with radicals). Topics 4–5 formalize it completely. Topics 6–8 apply it in geometry and statistics.
  • GED formula sheet: beginning with Topic 6, get students used to working with the official GED Mathematical Reasoning formula sheet exactly as it appears on the exam. Level 4 will complete this preparation.
  • TI-30XS calculator: used extensively at this level. Always remember to clear the memory before calculating. Practice these functions: √x, x², xⁿ, nPr, nCr, and π.
  • Continuity with Level 2 and Level 4: PEMDAS (L2) is applied without being explicitly mentioned. Factoring polynomials, quadratics, and systems of equations are reserved for Level 4.
  • Brightspace HTML: use solid hex colors only; native <details>/<summary> for the accordion; do not use rgba() or gradients. Remember not to use <label> in forms.
