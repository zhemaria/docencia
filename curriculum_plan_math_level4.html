How this plan is organized

Content is distributed across 8 major topics of 12 hours each (≈ 2 weeks per topic), following the Topical Outline v2 and the Student Learning Outcomes. This is the capstone level of the sequence: students who complete it are ready to take the GED® Mathematical Reasoning test.

Each topic contains 3 lessons of 4 hours. For every lesson: 📐 Materials/Resources, 💬 Guided practice, ✅ Quiz/Assignment and 📊 Rubric.

Topics 1–2 complete analytic geometry and advanced 3D volume. Topics 3–5 develop higher algebra (polynomials, quadratics, rational expressions). Topics 6–7 cover algebraic systems and the world of functions. Topic 8 integrates the modeling of quadratic and exponential functions with the final project and the GED practice test.

Topic 1 · Analytic geometry: the coordinate plane, slope, and equations of lines

Weeks 1–2 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: G · EE · SLO Geometry (Analytic) 1–3 · Sections 1.1 and 1.2 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 1.1 · The coordinate plane: ordered pairs, distance, and midpoint

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students graph ordered pairs in all four quadrants, calculate the distance between two points using the formula derived from the Pythagorean Theorem (d = √[(x₂-x₁)² + (y₂-y₁)²]), calculate the midpoint of a segment (M = ((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2)), and identify geometric figures in the coordinate plane.
Materials/Resources: grid paper with clearly marked axes; a map of Chicago on a grid to give context to "coordinates"; point cards for graphing; the distance and midpoint formulas derived from the Pythagorean Theorem (a link back to L3); MyOpenMath. Modality: the map as context before the mathematical abstraction.
Guided practice: "The city grid" — students work with a coordinate map of a fictional city: how far is the library from the hospital? what is the exact midpoint between two schools? Constructing figures: graph a triangle from three given points and calculate its perimeter using the distance formula. Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on graphing and identifying points, 3 on calculating the distance between two points, 2 on calculating the midpoint, 2 applied (the perimeter of a figure in the plane). TI-30XS calculator allowed. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: basic analytic geometry (3 criteria × 4 levels): graphs ordered pairs correctly in all 4 quadrants · applies the distance formula without substitution errors · calculates the midpoint correctly and interprets it geometrically.
▶

Lesson 1.2 · Slope: concept, formula, and interpretation

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students calculate the slope of a line from its graph (rise/run) and from two points (m = (y₂-y₁)/(x₂-x₁)); interpret slope as a rate of change ($ per hour, meters per second, cost per unit); identify lines with positive, negative, zero, and undefined slope; and compare the slopes of two lines to determine which is steeper.
Materials/Resources: graphs of real situations with interpretable slopes (gasoline price vs. liters, wages vs. hours); the quadrant rule for identifying the sign of the slope visually; the formula m = Δy/Δx; MyOpenMath. Modality: slope as "change per unit" before the algebraic formula.
Guided practice: "Ramp, price, and speed" — students calculate the slope in 6 real situations (an accessibility ramp, the price of electricity per kWh, the speed of a car in km/h) and interpret its meaning. Activity: given m = 3/5, how many different lines can have that slope? (infinitely many). What distinguishes them? (the intercept). Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on calculating slope from a graph, 3 from two points, 2 on identifying the type (positive/negative/zero/undefined), 2 on interpreting slope in context. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: slope (3 criteria × 4 levels): calculates the slope correctly (rise ÷ run; Δy ÷ Δx) · identifies the type of slope from the graph or from the points · interprets slope as a rate of change with units appropriate to the context.
▶

Lesson 1.3 · Equations of lines: slope-intercept, point-slope, and standard form

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students write the equation of a line in slope-intercept form (y = mx + b) given the slope and the intercept; use point-slope form (y - y₁ = m(x - x₁)) given a point and the slope or given two points; convert among standard form (Ax + By = C), point-slope, and slope-intercept; and write the equations of parallel and perpendicular lines.
Materials/Resources: table of the three forms of the equation of a line with examples of converting among them; the properties of parallel lines (equal slopes) and perpendicular lines (negative reciprocal slopes: m₁ × m₂ = -1); Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics; MyOpenMath. Modality: the form y = mx + b as the starting point; the others as transformations of it.
Guided practice: "The function GPS" — students receive data from a linear situation (taxi fare: $2.50 flag drop + $1.80 per km) and write the equation in all three forms. They then determine whether two lines are parallel, perpendicular, or neither. GED practice: 4 GED-style questions on equations of lines. Asynchronous: 12 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 12 questions in Brightspace — 3 on writing an equation given the slope and intercept, 3 given two points, 3 on converting among forms, 3 on parallel and perpendicular lines. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: equations of lines (4 criteria × 4 levels): writes y = mx + b identifying m and b correctly · applies point-slope form with correct substitution · converts among the three forms without algebraic errors · determines whether two lines are parallel or perpendicular using their slopes.

Topic 2 · Advanced 3D volume and introduction to polynomials

Weeks 3–4 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: G · A · SLO Geometry 5–6 · SLO Higher Algebra 1 · Sections 1.3 and 2.1 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 2.1 · Volume of spheres, cones, and pyramids

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students calculate the volume of spheres (V = 4/3 πr³), cones (V = 1/3 πr²h), and pyramids (V = 1/3 × base area × h) using the formulas from the GED formula sheet; solve for r or h when the volume is known; and solve applied problems (the capacity of a spherical tank, the volume of an ice cream cone, the amount of material for a pyramid).
Materials/Resources: the official GED Mathematical Reasoning formula sheet (the same one students will receive on the exam); the relationships V_cone = (1/3) V_cylinder and V_pyramid = (1/3) V_prism; TI-30XS calculator; MyOpenMath. Modality: use the GED formula sheet starting now — build the habit of locating the formula on the sheet before solving.
Guided practice: "Architecture and design" — students calculate: how many liters of water fit in a spherical tank with a radius of 1.2 m? how many cm³ of ice cream fit in a cone with a radius of 4 cm and a height of 10 cm? how many m³ of stone were used in the Great Pyramid of Giza (base 230 m × 230 m, height 146 m)? Asynchronous: 8 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on the volume of spheres, 3 on cones, 3 on pyramids, 1 on solving for r or h. The GED formula sheet and the TI-30XS calculator are allowed. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: advanced 3D volume (3 criteria × 4 levels): locates and applies the correct formula from the GED sheet · substitutes the dimensions correctly and calculates with π · states the result in cubic units with appropriate precision.
▶

Lesson 2.2 · Composite 3D figures and surface area

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students calculate the volume of composite 3D figures (prism + hemisphere, cylinder + cone, etc.) by adding or subtracting partial volumes; calculate the lateral surface area of cylinders, cones, and pyramids; and solve basic optimization problems (minimizing material for a given volume).
Materials/Resources: real images of farm silos (cylinder + cone), medicine capsules (cylinder + two hemispheres), coffee cups (truncated cylinder); the GED formula sheet; MyOpenMath; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: decompose the 3D figure into simple solids before calculating.
Guided practice: "The farmer's silo" — students calculate the total volume of a silo (a cylinder with a radius of 3 m and a height of 8 m + a cone on top with a height of 2 m) and how many tons of corn it holds (density provided). Surface area problem: how much paint do I need for the exterior of the cylinder? Asynchronous: 6 composite figure problems + 2 on surface area in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 8 questions in Brightspace — 4 on the volume of composite 3D figures, 2 on surface area, 2 on contextual applications (material cost, storage capacity). Calculator and formula sheet allowed. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: composite 3D figures (3 criteria × 4 levels): decomposes the figure correctly, identifying its parts · adds or subtracts the partial volumes correctly · calculates the surface area using the correct lateral formula.
▶

Lesson 2.3 · Polynomials: classification, addition, subtraction, and basic multiplication

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students classify polynomials (monomial, binomial, trinomial; degree of the polynomial); add and subtract polynomials by combining like terms; multiply a monomial by a polynomial using the distributive property; and evaluate a polynomial by substituting a numerical value.
Materials/Resources: terminology chart: degree of a term, degree of the polynomial, leading coefficient; polynomial cards for classification; MyOpenMath: polynomials lesson. Modality: the analogy with like terms in linear algebra — "you already know how to combine 3x + 5x; now you will combine 3x² + 5x²."
Guided practice: "The algebraic perimeter" — students express the perimeter of a polygon whose sides are given as polynomial expressions and simplify it. They then multiply monomial × binomial and monomial × trinomial in 6 exercises. Evaluation activity: what is the perimeter if x = 4? Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 2 on classifying and determining the degree, 3 on adding/subtracting polynomials, 3 on monomial × polynomial, 2 on evaluating with a given value. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: basic polynomials (3 criteria × 4 levels): classifies the polynomial correctly (name and degree) · combines like terms correctly in addition/subtraction · applies the distributive property in monomial × polynomial without sign errors.

Topic 3 · Multiplication of polynomials and factoring

Weeks 5–6 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: A · SLO Higher Algebra 1–2 · Sections 2.1 and 2.2 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 3.1 · Multiplying binomials: FOIL and special products

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students multiply binomials using the FOIL method (First, Outer, Inner, Last); apply the three special products: (a+b)² = a²+2ab+b², (a-b)² = a²-2ab+b², and (a+b)(a-b) = a²-b²; and recognize these patterns in given expressions in order to shorten the computation.
Materials/Resources: the area model (a rectangle divided into 4 parts) for visualizing FOIL before the algorithm; table of the three special products with a demonstration by FOIL; MyOpenMath. Modality: visual area model → mechanical FOIL → recognizing the special products in order to use the shortcut.
Guided practice: "Can I use a shortcut?" — students receive 12 products of binomials and classify them: general FOIL or a special product? They then apply the appropriate method. Verification: expand (x+5)² with FOIL and with the special product — do they give the same result? Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 12 questions in Brightspace — 5 on general FOIL, 3 on (a±b)², 4 on (a+b)(a-b). Time: 20 min. No calculator.
Rubric: multiplication of polynomials (3 criteria × 4 levels): applies FOIL correctly with all 4 products · identifies and uses the appropriate special product · simplifies by combining like terms in the result.
▶

Lesson 3.2 · Factoring: greatest common factor and difference of squares

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students factor by extracting the greatest common factor from all terms (identifying the GCF of the coefficients and the lowest exponent of each variable); factor differences of perfect squares (a²-b² = (a+b)(a-b)); and check the factorization by expanding to recover the original polynomial.
Materials/Resources: checking strategy: factor → expand → is it the same as the original? (always); list of perfect squares up to 225 for reference; MyOpenMath. Modality: factoring as the inverse of multiplication — always check by expanding.
Guided practice: "The factor detective" — students identify which type of factoring to apply (GCF or difference of squares) before factoring. 8 polynomials to factor and check. Connection activity: factor x²-25 → what value of x makes the result zero? (preparing for factoring trinomials). Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on the greatest common factor, 4 on the difference of squares, 2 of mixed type (GCF first, then difference of squares). Time: 20 min.
Rubric: basic factoring (3 criteria × 4 levels): extracts the GCF correctly (coefficient and variables) · recognizes and identifies the difference of squares · checks the factorization by expanding and recovering the original polynomial.
▶

Lesson 3.3 · Factoring trinomials (a=1 and a≠1)

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students factor trinomials of the form x²+bx+c by finding two numbers whose product is c and whose sum is b; factor trinomials of the form ax²+bx+c (a≠1) using factoring by grouping (or the ac product-and-sum method); and combine factoring techniques in expressions that require multiple steps.
Materials/Resources: a "two numbers that multiply to ___ and add to ___" table as an organizer; the ac method for a≠1 explained step by step; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics; MyOpenMath. Modality: reach fluency factoring with a=1 before moving on to a≠1. Always check by expanding.
Guided practice: "The factoring ladder" — Level 1: x²+7x+12, x²-5x+6. Level 2: x²-x-12, x²+3x-10. Level 3: 2x²+7x+3, 3x²-10x+8. Level 4: 4x²-12x+9 (a perfect square). Students advance through the levels at their own pace. Asynchronous: 10 trinomials of both types in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 with a=1 (with +, -, and mixed signs), 4 with a≠1, 2 on complete factoring (GCF first, then the trinomial). Time: 25 min.
Rubric: factoring trinomials (4 criteria × 4 levels): identifies the two numbers correctly for a=1 · applies the ac method or grouping for a≠1 · combines the GCF with trinomial factoring when necessary · checks the factorization by expanding.

Topic 4 · Quadratic equations

Weeks 7–8 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: A · SLO Higher Algebra 3–4 · Section 2.3 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 4.1 · Solving quadratic equations by factoring

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students solve quadratic equations of the form ax²+bx+c=0 by applying the Zero Product Property (if A·B=0, then A=0 or B=0): they put the equation in standard form, factor the trinomial or the difference of squares, apply the property, and check both solutions by substituting into the original equation.
Materials/Resources: the Zero Product Property explained with numerical examples before the algebraic ones; steps: (1) standard form ax²+bx+c=0; (2) factor; (3) set each factor equal to zero; (4) solve each linear equation; (5) check both solutions; MyOpenMath; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: the 5 steps as a mandatory procedure.
Guided practice: "Two solutions, one, or none by factoring?" — students solve 8 quadratic equations by factoring, including cases requiring rearrangement first (x²=9 → x²-9=0; x²=5x → x²-5x=0). Applied problem: the area of a rectangle whose length is 3 more than its width is 54. Asynchronous: 10 equations in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on solving directly, 4 requiring standard form first, 2 applied (area, trajectory). Time: 20 min.
Rubric: quadratic equations by factoring (4 criteria × 4 levels): puts the equation in standard form · factors correctly · applies the Zero Product Property to each factor · checks both solutions in the original equation.
▶

Lesson 4.2 · Completing the square

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students solve quadratic equations by completing the square: (1) put the equation in the form x²+bx = -c, (2) add (b/2)² to both sides, (3) write the left side as (x + b/2)², (4) take the square root of both sides with ±, (5) solve for x; and they understand that completing the square is the origin of the quadratic formula.
Materials/Resources: visual area model for completing the square (a square with a rectangle beside it and the "missing space" needed to complete it); laminated 5-step procedure; MyOpenMath. Modality: visual first, algebraic second; demonstrate that the quadratic formula is derived by completing the square on ax²+bx+c=0.
Guided practice: "From the figure to the algorithm" — students complete the square with the visual model for 2 equations, then apply the algebraic procedure to 6 equations. Derivation activity: in pairs, the instructor guides them step by step through the derivation of the quadratic formula by completing the square on the general equation. Asynchronous: 8 equations in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 8 questions in Brightspace — 4 on solving by completing the square (a=1), 2 with a≠1 (dividing by a first), 2 on identifying how much must be added to complete the square. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: completing the square (4 criteria × 4 levels): calculates (b/2)² correctly · maintains equality by adding to both sides · writes the perfect square trinomial as a squared binomial · takes the root with ± and solves correctly for x.
▶

Lesson 4.3 · The quadratic formula, the discriminant, and applications

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students apply the quadratic formula x = (-b ± √(b²-4ac)) / 2a to solve any quadratic equation; use the discriminant (b²-4ac) to determine in advance the number of real solutions (2, 1, or 0); and solve applied problems that model situations with quadratic equations (the trajectory of a projectile, area, geometry).
Materials/Resources: the quadratic formula from the official GED sheet; discriminant table (b²-4ac > 0 → 2 solutions, = 0 → 1, < 0 → 0 real solutions); TI-30XS calculator (for the discriminant and the root); Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: calculate the discriminant BEFORE solving — "how many solutions should I expect?"
Guided practice: "The projectile" — given h(t) = -16t²+96t+5 (height in feet, t in seconds), calculate: when does it hit the ground? when does it reach 120 feet? Classify 6 equations by their number of solutions using the discriminant before solving. Compare the 3 methods: when do I prefer factoring? when the formula? Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 12 questions in Brightspace — 3 on calculating and interpreting the discriminant, 5 on solving with the quadratic formula, 4 on contextual applications. Calculator and formula sheet allowed. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: the quadratic formula (4 criteria × 4 levels): identifies a, b, and c correctly · calculates the discriminant and interprets the number of solutions · applies the full formula without substitution errors · solves the applied problem and interprets the solutions in context.

Topic 5 · Complex rational expressions

Weeks 9–10 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: A · SLO Higher Algebra 5–7 · Section 2.4 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 5.1 · Simplifying rational expressions: factor and cancel

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students simplify algebraic rational expressions (fractions containing polynomials) by factoring the numerator and the denominator and canceling common factors; identify the excluded values (those that make the denominator zero) and state them alongside the simplified result.
Materials/Resources: analogy with numerical fractions: 12/18 = (2·6)/(3·6) = 2/3 → the same with polynomials; warning: only factors (multiplication) may be canceled, never terms (addition/subtraction); MyOpenMath. Modality: the numerical fraction as a model → algebraic fraction with monomials → fraction containing a trinomial.
Guided practice: "Which values do I exclude?" — students simplify 8 rational expressions and identify the values excluded from the domain. Trap error: (x+2)/(x²-4) → factor the denominator → can I cancel? Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on simplifying algebraic fractions, 4 on identifying excluded values, 2 on detecting incorrect cancellations. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: simplifying rational expressions (3 criteria × 4 levels): factors the numerator and denominator correctly · cancels factors only (not terms) · identifies every value excluded from the domain.
▶

Lesson 5.2 · Adding, subtracting, multiplying, and dividing rational expressions

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students multiply algebraic fractions (factor → cancel → multiply); divide (multiply by the reciprocal); add and subtract with identical denominators; and add and subtract with unlike denominators by finding the LCM of the polynomial denominators and rewriting with a common denominator.
Materials/Resources: procedure for addition/subtraction with unlike denominators (an exact parallel to numerical fractions); methods for finding the LCM of algebraic expressions; MyOpenMath. Modality: every operation is modeled first with numerical fractions, then with algebraic ones of the same type.
Guided practice: "Four algebraic stations" — the class rotates through 4 stations: (1) multiplying, (2) dividing, (3) adding with like denominators, (4) adding with unlike denominators. 3 problems per station. Pair work with cross-checking. Asynchronous: 12 mixed exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 12 questions in Brightspace — 3 on multiplication, 3 on division, 3 on addition/subtraction with like denominators, 3 with unlike denominators. Time: 30 min.
Rubric: operations with rational expressions (4 criteria × 4 levels): multiplies by factoring and canceling before multiplying · inverts correctly when dividing · finds the LCM of the denominators · adds/subtracts by rewriting with a common denominator without errors.
▶

Lesson 5.3 · Solving equations with rational expressions and modeling

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students solve equations containing algebraic fractions by multiplying both sides by the LCM of all the denominators to eliminate them, check that the solutions are not excluded values (extraneous solutions), and apply rational equations to modeling problems (work rates, mixtures, distance-rate-time variants).
Materials/Resources: the LCM method for "clearing" algebraic denominators; checking for extraneous solutions; 6 classic GED applied problems (working together: if A takes 3 h and B takes 4 h alone, how long together?); MyOpenMath; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: rational equation → multiply by the LCM → linear or quadratic equation → check.
Guided practice: "Working together?" — students solve 4 work-rate problems with the equation 1/a + 1/b = 1/t and 4 distance/speed problems with variations (with and against the current). They identify and discard extraneous solutions. Asynchronous: 8 problems in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on solving rational equations, 2 on identifying extraneous solutions, 4 applied (work, mixtures, distance). Calculator allowed. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: rational equations (4 criteria × 4 levels): multiplies correctly by the LCM to eliminate denominators · solves the resulting equation · verifies that the solutions are not excluded values · models and solves an applied problem with a rational equation.

Topic 6 · Systems of linear equations

Weeks 11–12 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: A · SLO Systems 1–5 · Section 3.1 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 6.1 · Solving systems of equations by substitution

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students solve systems of two linear equations in two variables using the substitution method: solve one equation for one variable, substitute into the other, solve for the first variable, and then calculate the second; they check the solution in BOTH original equations.
Materials/Resources: 5-step procedure: (1) solve for one variable; (2) substitute into the other equation; (3) solve for that variable; (4) substitute back to find the first; (5) check in both equations; context problems (two prices, two speeds); MyOpenMath. Modality: always choose the variable that is easiest to isolate (the one with a coefficient of 1 or -1).
Guided practice: "The price system" — two stores charge different prices for coffee and tea; from the data of two different purchases, students set up and solve the system to find the price of each product. 6 systems to solve by substitution. Asynchronous: 8 systems in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 8 questions in Brightspace — 3 on solving the system by substitution, 2 on verifying whether an ordered pair is a solution of the system, 3 on setting up and solving a contextual problem. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: substitution (4 criteria × 4 levels): solves for the variable correctly in the chosen equation · substitutes without sign errors · solves for both variables · checks the solution in both original equations.
▶

Lesson 6.2 · Solving systems by elimination and classifying systems

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students solve systems by the elimination method (linear combination): they multiply one or both equations so that the coefficients of one variable are opposites and eliminate it by adding; classify systems as independent (1 solution), inconsistent (0 solutions, parallel lines), or dependent (∞ solutions, the same line); and choose the most efficient method based on the structure of the system.
Materials/Resources: comparison table of substitution vs. elimination (when is each method easier?); system classification table with examples of all 3 types; MyOpenMath. Modality: eliminate the easiest variable (the one whose coefficients are opposites or easily manipulated).
Guided practice: "How many solutions does it have?" — students solve 6 systems and classify the result: do the lines intersect at a point, are they parallel, or are they the same line? Method-choice activity: given a system, do I eliminate or substitute? Justify in 2 sentences. Asynchronous: 10 systems in MyOpenMath (a mix of methods).
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on solving by elimination, 3 on classifying the system (type), 3 applied (mixtures, coins, speeds). Time: 25 min.
Rubric: elimination and classification (4 criteria × 4 levels): multiplies the equations by the correct factors to create opposites · eliminates the variable and solves · classifies the system correctly with justification · chooses the most efficient method for a given system.
▶

Lesson 6.3 · The graphing method, applications, and modeling with systems

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students solve systems of equations by graphing both lines on the same plane and reading the point of intersection; connect the graphical solution with the algebraic one; model real situations with systems of equations (price-demand equilibrium analysis, the break-even point of costs and revenue, mixture problems); and translate complex word problems into systems of equations.
Materials/Resources: grid paper for graphing; table of values for each line; modeling problems in economic and financial contexts; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics; MyOpenMath. Modality: graph first to visualize, then verify algebraically.
Guided practice: "The break-even point" — a company has fixed + variable costs and a revenue function; students graph both lines, find the break-even point, and interpret it: how many units must it sell to make money? Word problem: set up two systems from the text and solve them by the preferred method. Asynchronous: 6 systems in MyOpenMath, including 2 with graphs.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on identifying the solution of a system from its graph, 3 on setting up the system from a word problem, 4 on solving and modeling. Calculator allowed. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: modeling with systems (4 criteria × 4 levels): graphs both lines correctly and reads the point of intersection · connects the graphical solution with the algebraic one · correctly translates a word problem into a system of two equations · interprets the solution of the system in the context of the problem.

Topic 7 · Systems of inequalities and functions

Weeks 13–14 · 12 hours (6 synchronous + 6 asynchronous)
Standards: A · F · SLO Systems 6 · SLO Functions 1–3 · Sections 3.2, 4.1, and 4.2 of the Topical Outline
▶

Lesson 7.1 · Systems of linear inequalities: graphing the solution region

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students graph a linear inequality in the plane (solid or dashed line; shading the correct half-plane using a test point); graph a system of two or more inequalities and determine the feasible region (the intersection of the half-planes); verify whether a given point belongs to the solution region; and apply systems of inequalities to basic linear programming problems.
Materials/Resources: rule for distinguishing < (dashed) from ≤ (solid); the (0,0) test point method for choosing the correct side of the line; grid paper; MyOpenMath. Modality: graph each inequality separately → identify the intersection → verify a point.
Guided practice: "The production budget" — a company can produce x chairs and y tables subject to time and material constraints (a system of inequalities). Students graph the feasible region and determine which (x, y) combinations are viable. They test 4 candidate points. Asynchronous: 6 systems of inequalities in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 8 questions in Brightspace — 3 on graphing an inequality (identifying the correct graph), 3 on determining the feasible region of a system, 2 on verifying whether a point is a solution of the system. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: systems of inequalities (3 criteria × 4 levels): graphs the line correctly (solid vs. dashed) · identifies the correct half-plane using the test point · determines the feasible intersection region and verifies a point.
▶

Lesson 7.2 · Relations vs. functions, f(x) notation, and domain/range

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students distinguish relations from functions (a function assigns exactly one output value to each input); use the vertical line test to identify functions from a graph; use f(x) notation to evaluate functions; identify the domain and range of a function from a table, graph, or equation; and classify linear, quadratic, exponential, and rational functions by their form.
Materials/Resources: function machine diagram (input → rule → output); illustrated vertical line test; correspondence table with examples of functions and non-functions; MyOpenMath. Modality: the function machine as a metaphor before the formal definition.
Guided practice: "Function or not?" — students classify 10 relations (tables, graphs, mapping diagrams) as function/not a function. They then evaluate f(x) = 2x²-3x+1 for x = 0, 1, -2, 1/2. They identify the domain and range of 4 functions. Activity: compare f(3) vs. f(-3) across different types of function. Asynchronous: 10 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on identifying function/not a function (table, graph), 4 on evaluating f(x) for different types of function, 3 on domain and range. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: basic functions (3 criteria × 4 levels): applies the definition of a function correctly (the vertical line test and the table test) · evaluates f(x) by substituting and simplifying with PEMDAS · identifies the domain and range correctly from a graph, table, or equation.

Lesson 7.3 · Average rate of change and identifying types of functions

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students calculate the average rate of change of a function over an interval ([a,b]) as (f(b)-f(a))/(b-a) and interpret the result in context ($ per year, meters per second); distinguish linear functions (constant rate), quadratic functions (variable rate, parabolic shape), and exponential functions (multiplicative growth) by examining tables and graphs; and connect the slope of a line with the constant rate of change of a linear function.
Materials/Resources: comparison table of the three types of function: linear (constant difference in y), quadratic (constant second difference), exponential (constant ratio of change); the average rate of change formula; MyOpenMath; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: build tables for all three types and observe the pattern before the formal classification.
Guided practice: "Investment, free fall, and going viral" — students calculate the average rate of change in three situations: (1) a savings account with simple interest (linear), (2) the height of an object in free fall (quadratic), (3) a viral social media post (exponential). They classify 6 data tables by type of function. Asynchronous: 8 exercises in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on calculating the average rate of change over an interval, 3 on classifying the type of function from a table, 3 on interpreting the rate in context. Time: 20 min.
Rubric: rate of change (3 criteria × 4 levels): calculates (f(b)-f(a))/(b-a) correctly · interprets the result with units and meaning in context · classifies the function as linear, quadratic, or exponential based on the pattern of differences or ratios.

Lesson 8.1 · Quadratic functions: the parabola, the vertex, and the intercepts

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students graph quadratic functions f(x)=ax²+bx+c by identifying the vertex (x=-b/2a; y=f(-b/2a)), the axis of symmetry (x=-b/2a), and the intercepts with the axes (finding the x-intercepts with the quadratic formula; finding the y-intercept by setting x=0); determine whether the parabola opens upward (a>0) or downward (a<0); and use the vertex to solve maximum/minimum problems.
Materials/Resources: table of values for graphing parabolas; the connection between the quadratic formula (Topic 4) and the x-intercepts of the parabola; the vertex formula; grid paper; Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics; MyOpenMath. Modality: build the table → graph → identify the vertex and axis → confirm with the formula.
Guided practice: "The projectile and the profit" — (1) h(t)=-16t²+64t+5: graph it, find the maximum height (the vertex) and when it hits the ground (the x-intercepts); (2) P(x)=-2x²+120x-800: how many units maximize the profit? Asynchronous: 8 quadratic functions in MyOpenMath, including graphing and identifying key features.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 3 on calculating the vertex and the axis of symmetry, 3 on graphing and identifying key features, 4 on maximum/minimum problems (projectile, profit, maximum area). Calculator allowed. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: quadratic functions (4 criteria × 4 levels): calculates the vertex correctly (x=-b/2a → substitute) · identifies whether the parabola opens upward or downward · correctly finds the x-intercepts and the y-intercept · interprets the vertex as a maximum or minimum in the context of the problem.

Lesson 8.2 · Exponential functions, growth/decay, and an introduction to logarithms

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students graph and characterize exponential functions f(x)=a·bˣ (growth: b>1; decay: 0<b<1); apply exponential growth/decay in real contexts (compound interest, half-life, population growth); and are introduced to the logarithmic function as the inverse of the exponential (if b^y=x, then log_b(x)=y), connecting base-10 logarithms with the calculator.
Materials/Resources: table of values for y=2ˣ vs. y=f(x)=(1/2)ˣ to see growth vs. decay; the compound interest formula A=P(1+r/n)^(nt); an introduction to the logarithm as "to what exponent must the base be raised to obtain x?"; TI-30XS calculator (log key); MyOpenMath. Modality: the exponential function in a real context before the formal definition of the logarithm.
Guided practice: "The bank and time" — students calculate how $1,000 grows at 5% interest compounded annually over 1, 5, 10, and 20 years, graph the points, and describe the shape. Then: in how many years does it double? (an introduction to the logarithm as the answer). Calculate log₁₀(1000)=3 with the TI-30XS. Asynchronous: 6 exercises on exponential functions + 4 on basic logarithms in MyOpenMath.
Quiz: 10 questions in Brightspace — 4 on graphing/characterizing exponential functions, 4 on applied problems (compound interest, population, half-life), 2 introducing logarithms (what exponent...?). Calculator allowed. Time: 25 min.
Rubric: exponential functions (3 criteria × 4 levels): distinguishes growth from decay by the value of the base · applies the compound interest formula or an equivalent in a real context · connects the logarithmic function with the exponential as inverses.

Lesson 8.3 · Culminating integrative project + GED Mathematical Reasoning practice test GED

4 hours (2 sync. + 2 async.)
Objective: Students present the culminating integrative project "My Mathematical Model" (a real situation modeled with a quadratic or exponential function + a graph + algebraic analysis + a written interpretation), answer 10 advanced-level GED Mathematical Reasoning questions, and demonstrate command of the official GED formula sheet and the TI-30XS calculator under exam conditions.
Materials/Resources: the official GED Mathematical Reasoning formula sheet (used exactly as on the exam); template for the final project "My Mathematical Model"; a bank of 10 advanced-level GED-style questions (a mix of all Level 4 topics); GED strategy guide (time management, eliminating answer choices, using the formula sheet); Steck-Vaughn GED Mathematics. Modality: simulated exam conditions (timed, with only the permitted materials).
Guided practice: "It's exam time!" — Oral presentation of the project (3–4 min per student, with questions from the group). A 10-question GED-style practice test under real conditions (45 min, TI-30XS calculator and formula sheet only). Analysis of the results: which topics do I need to review? An individual final preparation plan. Asynchronous: students upload the project to Brightspace and complete the summative final assessment.
Quiz: Summative final assessment — 30 questions in Brightspace covering ALL topics from the 4 levels with emphasis on those of Level 4: 5 on analytic geometry and 3D volume, 5 on polynomials and factoring, 5 on quadratic equations, 5 on rational expressions, 5 on systems of equations, 5 on functions and modeling. Time: 90 min (the GED time limit). 1 attempt. TI-30XS calculator and the GED formula sheet are allowed.
Rubric: analytic rubric for the final project "My Mathematical Model" (5 criteria × 4 levels = 20 pts): (1) a real situation, well described and justified as quadratic or exponential · (2) a correct graph with all labels (axes, scale, units, name of the function) · (3) correct algebraic analysis (vertex, growth rate, value at a specific point) · (4) a coherent, complete written interpretation in the real context · (5) a clear, precise, confident oral presentation using appropriate mathematical vocabulary. The project grade (20 pts) is combined with the summative assessment for the final course grade.
📝 Pedagogical notes for review:
  • 50/50 distribution: each lesson = 2 sync. hours + 2 async. hours = 4 h. Total: 48 sync. + 48 async. = 96 h.
  • GED formula sheet: beginning with Topic 2, always use the official sheet exactly as it appears on the exam. The goal is for students to arrive at the GED already accustomed to using it efficiently, without losing time hunting for formulas.
  • TI-30XS calculator: this level makes extensive use of the advanced functions: x², √x, xⁿ, log, and basic graphing functions. Always remember: CLEAR THE MEMORY before each calculation.
  • Vertical coherence: the 4 curriculum plans (L1–L4) form a coherent system. Topic 1.3 of L4 (equations of lines) connects with Topic 5 of L3 (linear algebra). Topic 4 of L4 (quadratics) uses the factoring from Topic 3 of L4. Topic 8 integrates the entire system.
  • GED practice test in L8.3: this is the first time students face real exam conditions (timing, permitted materials, a single attempt). Prepare the group emotionally with a motivational talk before the practice test.
