Level 1 Mathematics: the foundations of number and reasoning

Spanish ASE Math · City Colleges of Chicago · NRS 1–2 · 96 hours · 16 weeks

Support document for the professional development session · Prof. José M. Fernández, ABE/ASE Math Master Teacher · Harry S. Truman College

Purpose of this report

This document accompanies the professional development presentation on Level 1 mathematics. It brings together, in a single place, the characteristics of the level, the topics that make it up, its role as the starting point of the sequence, its part in preparing students for Level 2, and a description of the integrative final project “My Graph of Life,” the first occasion on which students bring together and present everything they have learned. It is intended as a reference for faculty before, during, and after the session.

1What Level 1 is

Level 1 is the first stage of a single four-level sequence. It corresponds to NRS levels 1 and 2 and is the program’s point of entry: here the adult learner rebuilds number sense from the ground up —place value and the four operations— and takes the first steps toward mathematical reasoning. It is the level that turns a fragmented, half-remembered arithmetic into a solid, understood foundation.

2The identity of Level 1: three defining ideas

1 · Number is rebuilt from the ground up

Students work on place value and the four operations with whole numbers, decimals, and fractions, with understanding rather than memorized rules. For an adult who has been away from the classroom for years, a fragmented arithmetic becomes a firm foundation.

2 · Calculation begins to become reasoning

With patterns, a first algebraic language using simple unknowns, and one-step word problems, the habit of thinking —not just computing— takes hold. This is the seed of the mathematical reasoning that will mature in the levels that follow.

3 · Mathematics anchors in everyday life

Measurement, time, basic geometry (perimeter and area), and reading tables and graphs connect the subject to the tangible situations the adult learner already handles day to day.

3The eight topics of the level

Content is distributed across eight major topics of twelve hours each, following the progression of the Topical Outline in the 16-week syllabus and the Student Learning Outcomes. Topics 1–4 develop arithmetic (whole numbers, decimals, and fractions); topics 5–6 work on reasoning, patterns, and measurement; topics 7–8 close with geometry, data, and the final project.

4The connecting thread: the four operations, from whole numbers to fractions

The first four topics are not isolated blocks: they share one spine, the four operations. These are consolidated first with whole numbers, then extended to decimals and fractions, and from Topic 5 onward applied to measurement, geometry, and data. Mastering the operations here, with understanding, is what makes everything later possible.

The design avoids any abrupt jump: each new kind of number reuses operations the student already knows, so learners advance on familiar ground.

5The starting point: where students come from

As the first level, Level 1 does not build on a previous stage but on the adult learner’s life experience. The design rule is to start from what students already bring —often informally— and give it structure and understanding.

What students bring

  • Life experience and practical handling of money, time, and measurement.
  • Often years or decades away from the school system.
  • A fragmented arithmetic: rules memorized without understanding.
  • Intuitive notions of quantity, comparison, and sharing.
  • Concrete motivation: the GED, work, family.

What Level 1 builds

  • Place value and the four operations, with understanding.
  • Decimals and fractions from the concept, not the rule.
  • Patterns, first algebraic language, and simple unknowns.
  • Measurement, time, and applied conversions.
  • Basic geometry and reading and interpreting data.

6The arithmetic heart: decimals and fractions

Topics 3 and 4 —decimals and fractions— are the pivotal block of the level and, in large part, of the whole path toward the GED. It is where many adult learners have stumbled in the past, and for that reason the program treats it with special care and from real contexts.

The program’s approach in this block

The sequence always runs from concept to symbol: first the meaning (a part of a whole, a decimal place), then the procedure. Students work with fraction–decimal equivalences as two forms of the same quantity, with mixed numbers, and with word problems set in everyday situations (recipes, money, measurement). The TI-30XS calculator is introduced here, in Topic 3, not to replace calculation but to verify operations with decimals once the procedure is understood.

7The integrative final project: “My Graph of Life”

The level culminates, in lesson 8.3, with the integrative project “My Graph of Life” and an introduction to the GED format. It is the first time students bring together everything they have learned into a single task using their own data and present it to the group.

What students do

They build a graph from measurable personal data (for example, hours of sleep over five days, or the week’s expenses) and work through five elements:

The five elements of the Level 1 integrative final project and what students produce in each.
ElementWhat the student produces
1 · Data tableA complete, correct table of five measurable personal data points.
2 · Bar graphA well-labeled graph, with title, axes, and scale.
3 · Descriptive paragraphA paragraph with at least three data points read from their own graph.
4 · Basic calculationsTotal, average, and difference, calculated correctly.
5 · Oral presentationA 3–4 minute presentation to the group, with peer feedback.

Why it is a new way of applying what has been learned

It is the level’s first authentic integration: in a single task, students bring together arithmetic, calculation, graphical representation, and written and oral expression. The presentation to the group, with peer feedback (using sentence frames), develops mathematical communication, and the accompanying 10 GED-style questions offer a first contact with the exam format. The project grade (20 pts) is combined with the summative quiz to form the level’s final grade.

Submission allows for the oral presentation in the synchronous session and for posting the complete project in Brightspace, where each student comments on two peers’ work. It is assessed with a five-criterion analytic rubric, which respects different modes of expression (the UDL principle of multiple means of action and expression).

8Preparing for Level 2

Each Level 1 content area seeds a competency that Level 2 takes up and expands. The level does not end in itself: it builds the arithmetic base on which the work with signs, structure, and application will stand.

9Pedagogical notes for the instructor

  • Context before symbol. Each topic opens with a real situation —money, time, measurement, personal data— before formalizing. The symbol comes after the experience.
  • 50/50 distribution. Each lesson suggests 2 synchronous hours and 2 asynchronous: 48 and 48 in total, aligned with the four sections of the Topical Outline.
  • TI-30XS calculator. Introduced in Topic 3 to verify operations with decimals. Always remember to clear the memory before calculating.
  • Alignment with Steck-Vaughn. Each lesson can be linked to the pages of the GED Mathematics workbook for physical-book reference.
  • UDL design. Simple analytic rubrics (3–5 criteria × 3–4 levels) compatible with Brightspace Ally, authentic contexts, and multiple submission formats.
