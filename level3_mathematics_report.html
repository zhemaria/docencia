Level 3 Mathematics: The Level Where Algebra Becomes the Language of the Course

Spanish ASE Math · City Colleges of Chicago · AE 0170-0323 · NRS 3–4 · 96 hours · 16 weeks

Supporting document for the professional development session · Prof. José M. Fernández, ABE/ASE Math Master Teacher · Harry S. Truman College

Purpose of This Report

This document accompanies the professional development presentation on Level 3 Mathematics. It brings together, in a single place, the characteristics of the level, the topics that make it up, its continuity with Level 2, its role in preparing students for Level 4, and a detailed description of the culminating integrative project, «Mi análisis de datos» (My Data Analysis). It also includes a sample lesson — the Pythagorean Theorem — that illustrates the dual-modality model, in-person and online, on which the entire level has been built. It is intended as a reference for faculty before, during, and after the session.

1What Level 3 Is

Level 3 is the third segment of a single four-level sequence. It corresponds to NRS levels 3 and 4 and occupies the space between the proportional reasoning consolidated in Level 2 and the mathematics of functions and modeling in Level 4. It is the turning point of the entire program: the level at which students stop operating exclusively with numbers and begin to operate with symbols that represent numbers. Put another way, if Level 2 was the bridge to algebra, Level 3 is the moment of crossing it.

Each four-hour lesson retains the four components of the program model: materials and resources, guided practice, quiz or assignment, and rubric. 2The Identity of Level 3: Three Defining Ideas

1 · The Real Number System Is Completed

The first three topics close out the number system students have been building since Level 1. Here appear the complete rules of exponents (including the zero and negative exponents), scientific notation for handling the very large and the very small, and irrational radicals. With √2 and π placed on the number line, students see the complete set of real numbers for the first time: there are no gaps left.

2 · Algebra Stops Being "Arithmetic With Letters"

Topics 4 and 5 formalize linear algebra in its full extent: translation from verbal to symbolic language, properties of equality, equations with variables on both sides, special cases (no solution and infinitely many solutions), inequalities with their critical sign-change rule, and solving literal formulas for a given variable. Solving A = bh for h is the moment when the letter stops being an unknown to be "found" and becomes a quantity that can be operated on.

3 · Geometry Becomes Demonstrative

With the Pythagorean Theorem and its converse, similarity, and Thales' Theorem (the Basic Proportionality Theorem), geometry stops being a catalog of area formulas and becomes reasoning with justification. Students no longer simply calculate: they verify, check, and argue why their result is correct.

3The Eight Topics of the Level

Content is distributed across eight major twelve-hour topics, following the progression of the Topical Outline v2 of the 16-week syllabus and the Student Learning Outcomes. Topics 1–3 complete advanced number sense; topics 4–5 develop complete linear algebra; topics 6–7 cover spatial geometry and data analysis; topic 8 closes with combinatorics, compound probability, and the culminating integrative project.

4The Through-Line: From the Exponent to Algebra

The eight topics are not eight separate compartments: they form a single line of construction. The first three topics manipulate symbolic expressions — simplifying (2x³y⁻²)⁴ is already algebra, even if it is not yet called that — and in doing so they prepare the ground. When algebra is formalized in Topic 4, students have already spent six weeks working with letters without having noticed it. From Topic 6 onward, algebra becomes a tool: it is used to solve for a leg of a right triangle, to find the side of a similar triangle, and to find the radius of a cylinder from its volume.

This design avoids the classic error of presenting algebra as a separate subject that arrives all at once. Here the symbol is introduced through progressive familiarity and justified by usefulness: by the time students reach the formal linear equation, they already need it to solve problems they themselves have posed.

5Continuity With Level 2

Level 3 builds on what students already command and extends it with new content. The design rule is the same as throughout the program: what was learned in Level 2 is treated as consolidated and revisited only as a foundation; the new effort is concentrated on the symbol, on algebraic structure, and on geometric justification. PEMDAS, in particular, is no longer taught: it is applied without being mentioned, in every simplification at this level.

6The Geometry Block: The Sample Lesson

Topic 6 is the heart of the level and the one that best shows how the lessons have been built. Lesson 6.2, devoted to the Pythagorean Theorem, serves as a reference for faculty because in it, within a single four-hour session, all the features of the program's design are visible.

How Lesson 6.2 Is Built

The lesson opens by stating its objectives and an explicit connection to the previous lesson: in 6.1 the angles of the right triangle were studied; now the relationship among its sides is studied. That connection extends backward across the level — solving for a leg uses the algebra of Topics 4 and 5, and extracting the root uses Topic 3 — so that students perceive the course as a fabric rather than a list.

Content advances in short, verifiable steps: identifying the hypotenuse and the legs, calculating the hypotenuse, calculating a leg, recognizing the Pythagorean triples (3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25), and applying the converse to decide whether a triangle is a right triangle. Each block includes worked examples numbered step by step and warnings about the most frequent errors, such as adding when subtraction is called for while solving for a leg.

Interspersed throughout the text are interactive practice items with immediate feedback, along with a link to a visual resource on the GEDparaHispanos portal that allows students to manipulate the theorem. The lesson closes with the asynchronous assignment in MyOpenMath and its corresponding rubric.

Dual Modality: In-Person and Online

The most distinctive feature of the Level 3 lessons is that every guided practice activity is presented in two parallel versions: one for the in-person classroom and one for the online session. The visual demonstration of the theorem, for example, is done with paper squares constructed on each side of a 3-4-5 triangle in the classroom, and with the portal's interactive tool in the remote session. The activity "The Builder" solves eight real construction problems in class and four in small breakout rooms in pairs, recording the calculation on a shared sheet.

This is not an add-on: it is a design decision that responds to the reality of the program. Faculty can deliver the same lesson in either modality without redoing the planning, and students receive the same instructional experience regardless of how they attend. It is also a direct application of the UDL (Universal Design for Learning) principle of multiple means of representation and engagement.

7The Culminating Integrative Project: «Mi análisis de datos» (My Data Analysis)

The level culminates, in Lesson 8.3, with a culminating integrative project that replaces the traditional assessment as the closing of the course. As in Level 2, the project grade is combined with the summative quiz grade to form the final grade. The difference lies in the object of study: while the Level 2 project centered on the personal budget and central tendency, the Level 3 project focuses on the analysis of a complete distribution.

Students choose a real data set — personal, family, or work-related — and produce five elements:

The five components of the Level 3 culminating integrative project and what students produce in each.
Component What the Student Produces
1 · Data setA real data set, described with its source, its unit of measure, and its context.
2 · Graphical representationA box-and-whisker plot or a histogram, with all labels and its scale.
3 · StatisticsAt least three calculated measures (mean, median, interquartile range, or others) with the procedure shown.
4 · Written interpretationA coherent analysis of the shape of the distribution: symmetry, skew, and outliers.
5 · Probability calculationA probability calculation tied to the context of the student's own data.

Why This Project Closes the Level Well

The project requires students to travel through the entire level in a single task. Building the box plot requires the ordering and quartiles of Topic 7; calculating the mean requires consolidated arithmetic; expressing a probability as a fraction, decimal, and percentage requires the proportional reasoning of Level 2; and justifying in writing that a distribution is skewed requires the technical vocabulary acquired in the final two weeks.

The three- to four-minute oral presentation before classmates, with one required question from the audience, adds a communicative dimension uncommon in mathematics and highly valuable for adult learners. Submissions also accept alternative formats and are assessed with an analytic rubric of five criteria across four levels, which respects different styles of expression (the UDL principle of multiple means of action and expression). In Brightspace, each student comments on two classmates' projects, making peer assessment part of the learning itself.

8Preparation for Level 4

Every new content strand in Level 3 plants a competency that Level 4 takes up and extends all the way to the GED exam. Level 3 does not end in itself: it delivers to Level 4 a student who already thinks algebraically.

Two Deliberate Decisions About Scope

Factoring polynomials, quadratic equations, and systems of equations are expressly left outside Level 3 and reserved for Level 4. This is a conscious decision: attempting to cover them here would compromise the consolidation of linear algebra, which is the real objective of the level. Likewise, the GED formula sheet is introduced in Topic 6 and not before, so that students first understand where the formulas come from and only afterward learn to locate them quickly.

9Pedagogical Notes for the Instructor

  • Discovery before the rule. Several topics open by asking students to find the pattern before the rule is stated: the table of decreasing powers of 2 reveals on its own why a⁰ = 1 and what a negative exponent means. The rule arrives as a conclusion, not as an imposition.
  • Error as instructional material. Activities such as "The Exponent Referee" and "The Error Detector" present solutions with planted mistakes for students to identify and correct. Analyzing someone else's error is safer and more effective than waiting to commit it.
  • 50/50 distribution. Each lesson suggests 2 synchronous hours and 2 asynchronous hours: 48 and 48 in total.
  • Dual modality in every guided practice. Each activity has its in-person version and its online version, ready to use without prior adaptation.
  • TI-30XS calculator. It is used extensively at this level. It is worth practicing √x, x², xⁿ, the EE key for scientific notation, nPr, nCr, and π. Always remember to clear the memory before calculating.
  • GED formula sheet. Beginning with Topic 6, get students accustomed to using it exactly as it appears on the exam.
  • Alignment with Steck-Vaughn. Each lesson can be linked to the pages of the GED Mathematics workbook for reference to the physical book.
  • UDL design. Authentic contexts, immediate verification, dual modality, submission in multiple formats, and accessible materials in Brightspace with Ally compliance.
